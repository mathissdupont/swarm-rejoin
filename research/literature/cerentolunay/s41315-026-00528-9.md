**1 Introduction**


Robot localization is the problem of estimating a robot’s
pose over time with respect to a known or partially known
environment using noisy sensor observations and motion
commands. Reliable localization is a prerequisite for almost
every non-trivial autonomous behaviour: mobile robots
navigating warehouses and hospitals, agricultural platforms
operating in unstructured outdoor fields, assistive robots
collaborating with humans in homes, and autonomous
vehicles sharing the road with human drivers all depend on
accurate, robust, and timely estimates of where they are and
how they move.

From a systems perspective, localization couples three
tightly interacting components. First, _core estimation_
_algorithms_ fuse proprioceptive and exteroceptive mea­
surements into a consistent belief over the robot state, typi­
cally formulated in SE(2) or SE(3) (Sect. 2). Second, _map_

**2 From kinematics to cognition: core**
**estimation techniques in robot localization**


Core estimation algorithms lie at the heart of robot local­
ization: they transform noisy control inputs and sensor
measurements into a coherent belief over the robot state,
often jointly estimating aspects of the environment. This
section traces the evolution of these estimation techniques
from early odometry-based dead reckoning to probabi­
listic filtering, sample-based Bayesian estimators, graphbased smoothing, and learning-augmented architectures.
Throughout, we emphasize how design choices relate to the
challenges outlined in Sect. 1, such as drift accumulation,
perceptual aliasing, limited observability, scalability, and
robustness in dynamic environments.

From a functional standpoint, the methods reviewed here
answer the question of _how_ to fuse information from sensors
into consistent pose estimates, given a particular map repre­
sentation and sensor suite. Section 3 complements this view
by examining _what_ is represented about the environment
(metric, topological, or semantic structure), while Sect. 4
focuses on _which_ spatial cues different sensors provide
(relative motion, absolute position, geometry, semantics).
Practical localization pipelines emerge from the interplay
of these three pillars rather than from any component in
isolation.

**2.1 Odometry and proprioceptive methods**


Early mobile robots relied almost exclusively on proprio­
ceptive sensing to estimate motion. These methods infer
_relative_ pose changes from wheel rotations or inertial mea­
surements without explicit reference to external landmarks
or maps. Although simple and inexpensive, they are funda­
mentally limited by drift and unmodelled biases.

**Wheel odometry.** Wheel odometry integrates wheel
encoder counts to estimate linear and angular displacements
under kinematic assumptions about the drive configuration
(differential, synchro, Ackermann, omni-directional, etc.).
Classical work by Borenstein and co-workers (Borenstein
and Feng 1996; Borenstein 1995) systematically analysed
error sources such as unequal wheel diameters, wheel slip,
encoder quantization, and misalignment, and proposed prac­
tical calibration procedures and error models. Subsequent
studies refined these models and extended them to more
complex platforms and terrain conditions (Chong and Klee­
man 1997; Kelly 2001; Martinelli 2002; Lee et al. 2012).
Wheel odometry offers high-rate, low-latency motion esti­
mates that are cheap and energy-efficient, making it attrac­
tive for indoor mobile robots and AGVs.

However, the purely kinematic nature of wheel odometry

of 1– 2% of distance travelled is typical for differential-drive

and higher-level estimation algorithms.

**Inertial measurement units (IMUs).** Inertial measure­
ment units provide linear accelerations and angular veloci­
ties at high frequencies (typically 100–1000 Hz), enabling
dead-reckoning via strapdown inertial navigation (Titterton
and Weston 2004). IMUs capture fast, transient motions that
may be poorly sampled by other sensors and are therefore
crucial for agile platforms and for bridging short sensor out­
ages. When integrated in isolation, however, inertial naviga­
tion systems suffer from rapidly growing drift due to biases,
scale errors, and integration noise. High-grade navigation
IMUs can bound drift over several minutes, but are expen­
sive and heavy; low-cost MEMS IMUs commonly used in
mobile robots drift significantly within seconds (Titterton
and Weston 2004; Groves 2013).

Hybrid schemes that combine wheel odometry and
IMUs can partly compensate for the weaknesses of each
sensor (Lee et al. 2012). For example, angular rates from
the IMU can improve heading estimates during wheel slip,
while encoders help bound accelerometer bias growth dur­
ing low-dynamics phases. These proprioceptive fusions are
typically embedded within filtering or optimization frame­
works, anticipating the more general sensor fusion pipelines
discussed in Sects. 2.2 and 2.4 and revisited from a sensing
perspective in Sect. 4.

**Discussion.** Proprioceptive odometry methods are indis­
pensable for providing high-rate motion priors, but they can­
not address global consistency or perceptual aliasing on their
own. Their error characteristics directly motivate the intro­
duction of probabilistic filters that combine motion models
with exteroceptive observations to limit drift and maintain
consistent pose estimates over longer time horizons.


**2.2 Filtering-based estimators**

adoption of probabilistic filters that combine motion and
observation models to maintain an estimate of the robot

state and its uncertainty over time. Filtering-based estima­
tors represent the belief using a small number of parameters,
typically a Gaussian mean and covariance, and update these
parameters recursively as new data arrive.

**2.3 Bayesian and sample-based methods**


To overcome the limitations of parametric Gaussian filters
in highly nonlinear, multimodal settings, researchers turned
to non-parametric Bayesian estimators that represent the
belief explicitly over a discrete state space or via weighted
samples.

**Grid-based Bayesian estimation (Markov localiza­**
**tion).** Markov localization (Cox 1990; Koenig and Simmons
1996; Thrun 1998; Thrun et al. 2005) discretizes the robot’s
state space into a grid and maintains a probability for each
cell, updating the distribution with Bayes’ rule as actions
and observations arrive. Early work combined sonar or laser
range data with occupancy grid maps to perform global
localization and recovery from severe pose uncertainty,
such as the kidnapped robot problem. Grid-based methods
naturally handle strong nonlinearities and multimodality
(e.g., multiple symmetric hypotheses in highly aliased envi­
ronments), but they suffer from the curse of dimensional­
ity: the number of grid cells grows exponentially with state
dimension and resolution, limiting practical applications to
low-dimensional problems or relatively coarse grids.

**2.4 Graph-based and optimization-based**
**localization**


As environments and trajectories grew larger, the limita­
tions of purely filtering-based formulations became appar­
ent: linearization-induced inconsistency, difficulty revisiting
past decisions, and high cost for joint state–map estimation
with many landmarks. Graph-based and optimization-based
methods address these issues by representing localization
(and often mapping) as a sparse nonlinear least-squares
problem over a graph.

**Pose graphs and factor graphs.** In pose graph SLAM,
nodes represent robot poses and edges encode relative pose
constraints derived from odometry, loop closures, or other
sensors (Lu and Milios 1997; Howard et al. 2001; Olson
et al. 2006; Grisetti et al. 2010). More generally, factor
graphs represent the joint posterior as a product of factors,
each relating a small set of variables (poses, landmarks,
calibration parameters) (Dellaert 2012). This perspective

**Smoothing versus filtering.** Graph-based methods per­

SLAM, a map) by minimizing the global error induced by
all measurements (Dellaert and Kaess 2006; Bailey and
Durrant-Whyte 2006). In contrast to filtering, smoothing

2012) exploit the sparse structure of the normal equations
to update solutions efficiently in an online fashion. Modern
SLAM systems routinely combine filtering-style front-ends
(e.g., odometry and visual-inertial estimation) with graphbased back-ends for global consistency (Grisetti et al. 2007;
Hess et al. 2016; Campos et al. n.d.; Rosinol et al. 2020).

**Trade-offs and interaction with maps and sensors.**
Optimization-based methods offer excellent scalability and
consistency for large-scale problems, particularly when
combined with sparse linear solvers and careful marginal­
ization strategies. They naturally incorporate heterogeneous
constraints from diverse sensors and map types, including
metric maps, landmark-based representations, and seman­
tic or object-level information that yields additional fac­
tors (Grisetti et al. 2010; Dellaert 2012). However, they
generally rely on good initialization (often provided by
odometry, visual odometry, or inertial–GNSS filtering) and
can be sensitive to outliers, requiring robust cost functions
and loop-closure validation.

versus landmark graphs versus dense maps), while Sect. 4
discusses how specific sensor modalities (LIDAR, cameras,

problems.


**2.5 Learning-augmented localization estimators**


Recent advances in machine learning and deep neural
networks have motivated a range of learning-augmented
localization methods. Rather than replacing classical esti­
mators outright, most successful approaches use learning to
enhance specific components: feature extraction, measure­
ment models, data association, or prior representations.

**Learned measurement models and priors.** A common
pattern is to retain a classical filter or graph-based backend while replacing hand-crafted measurement models or
features with learned counterparts. Deep networks can, for
instance, predict dense depth maps or optical flow from
monocular images, which are then integrated into a visual
odometry or SLAM pipeline; or they can produce robust
place-recognition descriptors used to generate loop-closure



constraints (Walch et al. 2017; Sarlin et al. 2020; Mun et al.
2019). Other work learns priors over trajectories or maps,
biasing the estimator toward feasible motions or typical
scene layouts (Gellert and Schlögl 2018; Dourado et al.
2019).
**End-to-end pose regression.** End-to-end approaches
such as PoseNet (Kendall et al. 2016) train convolutional or
transformer-based networks to regress camera pose directly
from images, bypassing explicit feature extraction and geo­
metric optimization. Variants incorporate temporal models,
uncertainty estimation, and multi-task learning (Ching-Chih
et al. 2015; Xiao et al. 2019; Suzuki 2019). While these
methods can perform well in constrained environments and
offer appealing deployment simplicity, they often struggle
to generalize beyond the training distribution and provide
limited interpretability compared with classical geometrybased pipelines.

**Hybrid and semantic-aware estimators.** Hybrid sys­
tems combine probabilistic estimators with learned compo­
nents that provide semantic or contextual information. For
instance, semantic segmentation networks can label objects
and surfaces in the environment, and these labels can be
incorporated into the map representation or used to modu­
late sensor models, improving robustness under appearance
changes and dynamic objects (Zou et al. 2019; Chen et al.
2023). Recent work explores integrating language-condi­
tioned priors and high-level reasoning, where LLMs and
VLMs contribute semantic constraints or task-level context

to the localization process (Choi et al. 2025). These trends
blur the line between purely geometric localization and
more cognitive forms of spatial understanding.

**Discussion.** Learning-augmented estimators hold prom­
ise for improving robustness, adaptability, and semantic
awareness, particularly in challenging environments where
hand-crafted models are brittle. At the same time, they intro­
duce new challenges related to data requirements, general­
ization, uncertainty quantification, and safety. In this survey,
we treat learning-augmented methods as complementary to
classical probabilistic and optimization-based techniques:
they enrich the information available to the estimator and
can modify its behaviour, but they rarely obviate the need
for principled state estimation. Section 3 further discusses
how learned representations influence map design, while
Sects. 4 and 5 revisit learning-based sensing and reasoning
in the broader context of future localization systems.

**3 Building the world: map representations**
**in localization**


Localization cannot occur in a vacuum: it must be grounded
in some representation of the environment. In this survey,
_map representations_ are understood as any spatial struc­
tures that allow an estimator to relate sensor measurements

and control inputs to a consistent notion of place. These
maps may encode metric geometry, topological connectiv­
ity, semantic labels, or learned latent structure. Unlike pure
mapping surveys, our focus here is not on map construction
alone, but on how different map structures support the _local­_
_ization_ algorithms discussed in Sect. 2 and interact with sen­
sor modalities in Sect. 4.


From a functional point of view, map-based localization
answers the question of _where am I within a given repre­_
_sentation of the world?_ Metric maps (e.g., occupancy grids,
point clouds, dense depth maps) support geometric align­
ment of new measurements to a reference frame. Feature

and keyframe maps support correspondence-based and
appearance-based localization. Topological and semantic
maps provide a more abstract notion of place, useful for
large-scale navigation and task-level reasoning. Learningaugmented and implicit representations further extend these
ideas by embedding geometry and semantics into neural
networks or other learned models.


_**Taxonomic note.**_ In line with the taxonomy introduced in
Sect. 2, we group map-based localization methods according
to the _dominant spatial abstraction_ used by the estimator:


- _Laser-based metric map localization_ (Sect. 3.1) oper­
ates on dense geometric representations such as occu­
pancy grids, height maps, or point clouds, typically de­
rived from 2D or 3D LIDAR scans.

- _Vision-based geometric and feature-based localization_
(Sect. 3.2) uses sparse or semi-dense feature maps, key­
frame graphs, or direct image-to-map alignment based
on cameras and depth sensors.

- _Topological and semantic map-based localization_
(Sect. 3.3) leverages place graphs, appearance-based
indices, and semantic or object-centric maps to reason
about locations at a higher level of abstraction.

- _Learning-augmented and implicit map representations_
(Sect. 3.4) integrate neural or other learned representa­
tions into the map structure itself, often combining ge­
ometry and semantics in a common latent space.

**3.1 Laser-based metric map localization**


Laser-based localization techniques rely on aligning LIDAR
scans against a metric representation of the environment,
such as an occupancy grid, polygonal map, or point cloud.
These methods exploit the high range accuracy and angular
resolution of modern LIDAR sensors and remain a corner­

stone of many commercial autonomous systems.

A foundational approach is the Iterative Closest Point
(ICP) algorithm (Besl and McKay 1992), which iteratively
matches points between a new scan and a reference point
cloud to recover the rigid transform that minimizes align­
ment error. Variants such as point-to-plane ICP and gen­
eralized ICP (Segal et al. 2009) improve convergence and
robustness by incorporating surface normals and proba­
bilistic error models. In parallel, correlative scan match­
ing (Olson 2009) aligns scans by evaluating discrete pose
hypotheses over a search window using precomputed occu­
pancy or likelihood grids, trading computation for stronger
robustness to local minima.


An alternative formulation, the Normal Distributions
Transform (NDT) (Biber and Strasser 2003; Magnusson
2009), represents the map as a set of Gaussian distributions
over grid cells. Localization then amounts to maximizing
the likelihood of the observed scan under this continuous

approximation. Multi-resolution and adaptive-grid vari­
ants (Hornung et al. 2013) improve robustness in sparse,
noisy, or large-scale environments by focusing computation
on informative regions.

In practice, laser-based metric map localization typically
appears as a _front-end_ that provides relative pose constraints
to a filtering or graph-based _back-end_ (Sect. 2). Scan-tomap alignment yields odometric constraints, while loop
closures arise from aligning scans in revisited areas. The
choice of map representation (e.g., occupancy grid versus
NDT versus point cloud) influences both accuracy and com­
putational cost, as well as sensitivity to dynamic objects and
structural changes.

Overall, laser-based metric methods offer high localiza­
tion accuracy and strong robustness in static or moderately
dynamic environments, particularly when dense geometry
is available. Their main limitations are the reliance on suf­
ficient geometric structure, reduced performance in highly

**3.2 Vision-based geometric and feature-based**
**localization**


Vision-based localization techniques operate on maps
derived from cameras and, increasingly, RGB-D or stereo
sensors. These maps typically encode sparse visual fea­
tures, semi-dense photometric information, or dense depth,
organized as keyframes and landmarks in a graph structure.
Compared with LIDAR, cameras are inexpensive and pro­
vide rich texture and semantic information, but they are
more sensitive to lighting changes, motion blur, and texture­
less surfaces.


Classical feature-based visual localization constructs a

sparse landmark map by detecting and matching keypoints
(e.g., SIFT, SURF, ORB) across images and estimating
camera poses that are consistent with these correspon­
dences (Scaramuzza et al. 2006; Matsuo et al. 2014). The
resulting maps are well suited for incremental SLAM and
revisiting previously seen places via appearance-based loop
closure (Cummins and Newman 2008; Galvez-López and
Tardós 2012). Geometric consistency checks and robust
estimators (e.g., RANSAC, bundle adjustment) provide
resilience against outliers.

Direct and semi-direct visual methods dispense with
explicit feature descriptors and instead minimize photomet­
ric error between images or between images and a stored
keyframe map (Newcombe et al. 2011; Engel et al. 2014).
These approaches exploit more of the image information
and can be more robust in low-texture settings, but they
depend on stable illumination and accurate photometric cal­
ibration. Depth cameras and stereo rigs further augment the
map with dense or semi-dense geometry, improving scale
observability and enabling more accurate alignment.

Vision-based maps are tightly coupled with the estima­
tor design. For instance, keyframe–landmark maps naturally
support graph-based SLAM back-ends that optimize camera
poses and landmark positions, while semi-dense maps can
be used as implicit surfaces for camera tracking. Cameras
are also central to semantic and learning-augmented maps
(Sects. 3.3 and 3.4), where visual features are combined
with higher-level labels and learned representations.

Compared with laser-based metric maps, vision-based
representations offer lower sensor cost and richer semantics
but are more prone to appearance changes and may provide
weaker geometric constraints in texture-poor environments.
Hybrid LIDAR–vision maps seek to combine the strengths
of both, using LIDAR for robust geometry and vision for
long-range appearance-based loop closure and semantics.

**3.3 Topological and semantic map-based**
**localization**


Topological and semantic maps abstract away from pre­
cise metric geometry to represent the environment as a
graph of places and their relationships. Nodes correspond
to locations, regions, or semantic entities (e.g., rooms, cor­
ridors, objects), and edges encode navigability, adjacency,
or logical relations. Localization then amounts to inferring
the most likely node or set of nodes given current sensor
observations.


Appearance-based topological maps represent each place
using visual or LIDAR-derived descriptors and organize
them in a retrieval structure for loop closure and place rec­
ognition (Cummins and Newman 2008; Galvez-López and
Tardós 2012). These maps scale gracefully to large envi­
ronments because they store only a subset of keyframes
or descriptors and can employ efficient indexing. Metric
information is often maintained in a separate layer (e.g., a
pose graph) while the topological layer captures long-range
connectivity, enabling robust global localization and multisession mapping.

Semantic maps enrich metric or topological structures
with labels and object instances inferred from percep­
tion (Mccormac et al. 2017; Bowman et al. 2017). For
example, SemanticFusion and related approaches couple
dense SLAM with semantic segmentation networks to
build maps where each surface element is associated with
a semantic class. Other works build object-centric or roomcentric maps that capture the layout of furniture, walls, and
functional areas. In these settings, localization can exploit
both geometry and semantics, improving robustness under
appearance change (e.g., lighting, weather) and support­
ing higher-level reasoning such as “in the kitchen near the
fridge”.

Topological and semantic map-based localization inte­
grates naturally with the estimator families of Sect. 2.
Graph-based back-ends can treat places and semantic enti­
ties as additional nodes with factors that encode co-occur­
rence or relative relationships. Particle filters can maintain
multi-hypothesis beliefs over topological nodes in ambigu­
ous environments, while learning-augmented estimators can
provide semantic descriptors and language-conditioned pri­
ors that connect human instructions to locations.


The key advantages of these representations are scal­
ability, interpretability, and robustness to certain types of
appearance change. Their limitations include reduced metric
precision (unless combined with a metric layer), sensitivity
to semantic prediction errors, and the need for substantial
training data when deep semantic models are used.



**3.4 Learning-augmented and implicit map**
**representations**


Learning-augmented map representations incorporate neu­
ral or other learned models into the map structure itself.
Rather than storing geometry and semantics as explicit grids
or graphs alone, these methods represent the environment
via neural fields, latent codes, or parameterized functions
that are optimized from data.

Implicit neural representations, such as neural occu­
pancy fields and radiance fields (NeRF), model the envi­
ronment as a continuous function mapping coordinates (and
possibly viewing directions) to density, color, or seman­
tic labels (Mildenhall et al. 2020; Mescheder et al. 2019).
Localization can then be framed as finding the pose whose
rendered view best matches the current observation. While
these approaches yield high-fidelity reconstructions and
compact representations of complex scenes, they often
require substantial training time and can be computationally
intensive at inference, limiting their current use to offline or
slowly changing environments.

Other learning-augmented maps use neural networks to
compress or regularize classical structures. For example,
learned place-recognition descriptors provide a semanticand appearance-aware index over keyframes or LIDAR
scans (Sarlin et al. 2020; Walch et al. 2017), effectively
functioning as a topological layer atop a metric map. Neural
networks can also predict occupancy or traversability from
raw sensor data, building semantic occupancy grids that
better capture free space and dynamic objects than classical
inverse sensor models.


These representations are closely intertwined with the
learning-augmented estimators of Sect. 2.5: the same net­
work that provides a map can also serve as a learned mea­
surement model or prior. The resulting systems blur the
distinction between mapping and localization, as the map
is no longer a static structure but part of a learned function
that supports both tasks. This flexibility comes at the cost of
data requirements, generalization challenges, and difficul­
ties in quantifying uncertainty and ensuring safety-critical
robustness.

**4 From sensors to situational awareness: a**
**functional view of localization modalities**


Localization is ultimately constrained by what the robot can
sense. While Sects. 2 and 3 focused on how estimation algo­
rithms and map representations transform sensor data into
pose estimates and spatial models, this section examines the
complementary question of _what_ spatial information differ­
ent sensor modalities provide, _how_ it is acquired, and _for_
_which roles_ in the localization pipeline. Rather than group­
ing sensors purely by hardware type, we adopt a functional
taxonomy based on the spatial cues they contribute: relative
motion increments, globally anchored pose, environmental
geometry, and semantic or contextual information.

Accordingly, we distinguish four broad categories. Rela­
tive pose sensors (Sect. 4.1) provide high-rate egomotion
increments but suffer from drift. Global positioning sensors
(Sect. 4.2) deliver absolute pose anchoring where infra­
structure or satellite coverage is available. Environmental
geometry sensors (Sect. 4.3) reconstruct the surrounding
structure to support map-based localization and loop clo­
sure. Finally, semantic perception modules (Sect. 4.4) infer
higher-level information from raw signals, such as object
classes or room types, and thus contribute contextual cues
rather than directly measuring new physical quantities.
Section 4.5 summarizes the resulting trade-offs and relates
these modalities back to the estimator and map taxonomies
in Sects. 2 and 3.



**4.1 Relative pose estimation sensors**


Relative pose estimation sensors provide continuous, highrate estimates of local motion by integrating incremental
measurements over time. They are indispensable for shortterm stability, control, and bridging between sparser global
or map-based updates, particularly when external references
such as GNSS or pre-built maps are unavailable or intermit­
tent. However, by construction they cannot recover abso­
lute pose and suffer from unbounded drift if not regularly
corrected.


Wheel encoders remain one of the most widely used rela­
tive sensors for indoor and ground-based robots. By inte­
grating wheel rotation under a kinematic model of the drive
configuration, wheel odometry provides inexpensive, lowlatency estimates of planar motion (Borenstein and Feng
1996; Borenstein 1995). As discussed in Sect. 2.1, system­
atic errors (unequal wheel diameters, misalignment) and
non-systematic effects (wheel slip, surface irregularities)
lead to drift on the order of 1– 2% of distance travelled even

under favourable conditions (Borenstein and Feng 1996;
Thrun et al. 2005). In the sensor taxonomy, encoders are
therefore best seen as high-rate, local motion cues that must
be fused with exteroceptive information using the estima­
tors from Sect. 2 and maps from Sect. 3.

Inertial measurement units (IMUs) provide linear accel­
erations and angular velocities at rates of up to several
hundred hertz, enabling strapdown inertial navigation (Tit­
terton and Weston 2004; Groves 2013). High-grade IMUs
can maintain accurate short-term motion estimates even

under aggressive manoeuvres, making them crucial for
aerial vehicles and legged robots (Aggarwal et al. 2008;
Shin 2005). Low-cost MEMS IMUs commonly used in
mobile robots, however, exhibit substantial bias drift and
noise, causing position errors to grow rapidly when inte­
grated in isolation (Groves 2013; Mohamed et al. 2019).
In practice, IMUs serve as complementary high-dynamics
sensors whose biases and drift are continuously corrected
using other modalities within filtering or optimization
frameworks.


Visual odometry (VO) estimates relative motion from
image sequences by tracking features or minimizing photo­
metric error (Scaramuzza and Fraundorfer 2011). Featurebased methods detect and match keypoints between frames
and use multi-view geometry to recover camera pose (MurArtal and Tardos 2017), while direct and semi-direct meth­
ods operate directly on image intensities (Engel et al. 2014).
VO provides rich motion cues using inexpensive cameras
and is particularly attractive in GNSS-denied environments.
However, it remains sensitive to illumination changes,
motion blur, and texture-poor scenes, and, like other rela­
tive modalities, accumulates drift over time.

sufficient for long-term localization on their own. They are
most effective when paired with global positioning, geomet­

lute or loop-closing information.


**4.2 Global positioning sensors**


Global positioning sensors provide absolute pose esti­
mates by relating local measurements to external references
anchored in a global frame. They are particularly valuable
for large-scale outdoor navigation, multi-robot coordina­
tion, and as global priors for SLAM and map-building.

Global Navigation Satellite Systems (GNSS), including
the United States’ Global Positioning System (GPS), Rus­
sia’s GLObal NAvigation Satellite System (GLONASS),
Europe’s Galileo, and China’s BeiDou constellations deter­
mine receiver position by trilateration from pseudorange
measurements to multiple satellites (Groves 2013). Stan­
dard code-based receivers typically provide meter-level
accuracy in open-sky conditions. Carrier-phase techniques
such as Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) and Precise Point
Positioning (PPP) achieve centimetre-level accuracy by
resolving integer ambiguities using one or more reference
stations (Groves 2013; Ahmed et al. 2025). However, GNSS
performance degrades in urban canyons, tunnels, forests,
and indoor environments due to signal blockage, multipath,
and ionospheric effects.

Ultra-wideband (UWB) radio systems offer an attrac­
tive complement to GNSS in indoor and GPS-denied
settings (Alarifi et al. 2016; Al-Okby et al. 2024). By mea­
suring time-of-flight or time-difference-of-arrival between
mobile tags and fixed anchors, UWB can provide decime­
tre-level ranging and localization even in cluttered environ­
ments (Zhao et al. 2020). UWB is robust to many forms
of multipath and interference, but requires infrastructure
deployment and careful calibration of anchor positions.

In indoor environments where GNSS signals are unavail­
able or severely attenuated, infrastructure-based systems
can provide absolute position anchors within a bounded

**4.3 Environmental geometry sensors**


Environmental geometry sensors enable robots to perceive
the shape and structure of their surroundings, providing the
basis for map-based localization, obstacle avoidance, and
loop closure. The dominant modalities in this category are
LIDAR and RGB-D cameras, though acoustic sonars, stereo
rigs, and event cameras also contribute in specific niches.

LIDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) sensors emit laser
pulses and measure their return time to obtain dense range
scans. Two-dimensional LIDAR has long been used with
occupancy grid maps and scan-matching techniques for
robust indoor localization (Thrun et al. 2005; Olson 2009).
Three-dimensional spinning and solid-state LIDARs extend
this principle to full 3D structure, supporting odometry and
SLAM in complex outdoor environments (Zhang and Singh
2014; Shan and Englot 2018). Modern LIDAR odometry
and mapping systems exploit geometric primitives such as
planes and edges, combined with graph-based back-ends, to
achieve high accuracy and robustness at moderate compu­
tational cost.


RGB-D cameras, such as the Microsoft Kinect and
Intel RealSense, provide aligned colour and depth images
at video rates. They enable dense or semi-dense SLAM
in structured indoor spaces by fusing depth measurements
into voxel grids, surfel maps, or truncated signed distance
fields (Endres et al. 2012; Cadena et al. 2016). Compared
with LIDAR, RGB-D sensors are compact and relatively
inexpensive, but offer a limited range and field of view and
are sensitive to ambient lighting and reflective surfaces.

Geometry sensors are tightly coupled to the map repre­
sentations of Sect. 3. LIDAR and RGB-D measurements

**4.4 Semantic perception and high-level cues for**
**localization**


Semantic perception does not constitute a separate physical
sensing modality; instead, it denotes the set of processing
modules that infer high-level information—such as object
classes, room categories, or road semantics—from raw sen­
sor signals. These modules operate on images, depth maps,
point clouds, or other measurements to produce semantic
labels, object instances, or relational structure that can sup­
port localization and mapping at a more abstract level.

A prominent example is SemanticFusion, which couples
dense RGB-D SLAM with real-time semantic segmentation
to build maps where each surface element is labelled with
a semantic class (Mccormac et al. 2017). Variants extend
this idea to object-centric and room-centric maps, enabling
robots to reason about “being in the kitchen” or “near the
table” rather than solely in metric coordinates (Bowman
et al. 2017; Michael et al. 2022). Other work combines lidar
or multi-modal data with deep networks to derive seman­
tic point clouds, lane markings, or traversability estimates
for autonomous driving and outdoor navigation (Jiang et al.
2023; Chen et al. 2021).
In the context of this survey, semantic perception serves
two main roles. First, it enriches the map representations of
Sect. 3.3 with labels and object instances, thereby enabling
semantic map-based localization and topological reason­
ing. Second, it provides high-level cues to the learning-aug­
mented estimators of Sect. 2.5, for example by suggesting
loop-closure candidates based on object or scene similarity,
or by conditioning motion priors on the current room type
or task.

**5 Trends, challenges, and future directions**


The preceding sections reviewed core estimation techniques
(Sect. 2), map representations (Sect. 3), and sensor modali­
ties (Sect. 4) through a functional lens. Taken together, they
show that modern localization is no longer a single algorith­
mic choice, but a system-level design problem that couples
estimators, multi-layer maps, and heterogeneous sensors.

This section synthesizes cross-cutting trends that are
reshaping the field and identifies open challenges that
remain even in state-of-the-art systems. Rather than
attempting an exhaustive list, we focus on directions that

**5.1 Emerging research trends**


The landscape of robot localization is being reshaped by
advances in machine learning, representation learning, and
distributed systems, as well as by the increasing deployment
of robots in complex, human-centric environments. Several
trends are particularly prominent.

**Learning-augmented estimation and active localiza­**
**tion.** Section 2.5 highlighted how learning is increasingly
used to enhance, rather than replace, classical estimators.
Learned measurement models, priors, and semantic cues
are now routinely integrated into filters and graph-based
back-ends, improving robustness to appearance change
and complex sensor characteristics. A related trend is
_active localization_, in which planning and estimation are
co-designed: the robot selects actions that are informative
for localization, for example by trading off exploration and
exploitation or by explicitly planning to disambiguate per­
ceptual aliasing. Combining learning-augmented estimators
with principled active policies remains a central opportu­
nity, especially in environments where observations are
expensive or safety-critical.

**Implicit and multi-layer map representations.** As
reviewed in Sects. 3.3 and 3.4, there is growing interest in
maps that go beyond a single metric grid or point cloud.
Multi-layer maps combine local metric submaps, topo­
logical place graphs, and semantic or object-centric layers,
enabling systems to operate at multiple levels of abstrac­
tion. In parallel, implicit neural representations and neural
fields offer compact, continuous encodings of geometry and
appearance. A major trend is the integration of these struc­
tures: classical metric and topological maps provide strong
geometric priors and reliability, while learned and implicit
layers capture fine-scale details, semantics, and long-range
correlations. Designing estimators that can consistently rea­
son across such layered maps is an active research frontier.

**Cross-modal, cross-embodiment, and multi-robot**
**localization.** Section 4 emphasized that no single sensor
family is sufficient across all regimes. Recent work there­
fore focuses on cross-modal localization, where systems
exploit overlapping structure between different sensor suites
(e.g., LIDAR-only and camera-only platforms) and transfer



localization capabilities across robots or datasets. At the
same time, multi-robot SLAM and distributed localization
architectures aim to share information efficiently between
agents while respecting bandwidth and privacy constraints.
These trends point toward localization systems that can
adapt to heterogeneous platforms and dynamically changing
sensor configurations without extensive manual retuning.

**Resource-aware and safety-critical localization.** As
localization moves from research prototypes to deployed
products, constraints on computation, energy, and safety
become central. Resource-aware localization seeks to

maintain reliable performance on embedded platforms by
adapting estimator complexity, map resolution, and sensing
rates to available resources. Neuromorphic and event-based
sensing, hardware acceleration, and approximate inference
methods are being explored to reduce latency and power
consumption. At the same time, safety-critical domains such
as autonomous driving, aerial inspection, and collaborative
manipulation demand rigorous treatment of uncertainty,
fail-safe behaviours under localization loss, and interfaces
to formal verification and certification processes.

**Semantics, language, and human-centric localization.**
Finally, there is a growing interest in localization systems
that can reason not only about coordinates, but also about
human-interpretable concepts such as rooms, objects, and
tasks. Semantic and object-centric maps (Sect. 3.3) provide
a substrate for this, while VLMs and LLMs offer mecha­
nisms for grounding natural-language descriptions and
instructions in the robot’s spatial representation. Early work
explores using such models for semantic loop closure, scene
description, and language-guided navigation, but robustly
integrating high-level, symbolic reasoning with low-level
geometric estimation remains an open and challenging
direction.


**5.2 Open challenges and future directions**


Despite substantial progress, several fundamental chal­
lenges remain. We group them here into five themes that cut
across estimators, maps, and sensing.

**Robustness in extreme and dynamic environments.**
Many localization systems still struggle in the presence of
severe dynamics (moving crowds, traffic, foliage), adverse
weather and lighting, or structural changes. While robust
cost functions, outlier rejection, and semantic filtering
help, achieving sustained performance under such condi­
tions remains difficult. A key open question is how to for­
mally characterize and guarantee robustness when both the

## `1 3`

## PDF page 18

812


environment and the sensor suite may deviate significantly
from the conditions seen during development or training.

**Scalability and computational efficiency.** Large-scale,
long-term deployments generate vast amounts of data and
require estimators and map structures that can grow grace­
fully without sacrificing real-time performance. Sparse
graph-based formulations, submapping, and hierarchical
maps address part of this challenge, but trade-offs between
map resolution, memory, and estimator complexity are still
poorly understood. There is a need for principled strategies
to adaptively select what to store, what to marginalize, and
what to discard, while preserving the information most rel­
evant for future localization and planning.

**Data efficiency, generalization, and evaluation.**
Learning-augmented estimators and semantic maps require
substantial training data, which may not be available for
every environment or sensing configuration. Methods that
can transfer across domains, leverage self-supervision, or
exploit simulation-to-real pipelines are promising but not
yet mature. In parallel, the community lacks standardized,
cross-modal benchmarks that systematically probe failure
modes, long-term drift, and recovery from localization loss
across different estimator, map, and sensor combinations.
Developing such benchmarks and evaluation protocols is
essential for comparing methods and for supporting deploy­
ment in safety-critical settings.

**Uncertainty quantification, verification, and inter­**
**pretability.** Classical probabilistic estimators provide
explicit uncertainty representations, but these are often
violated in practice due to modelling approximations, lin­
earization, and unmodelled correlations. Learning-based
components further complicate the picture, as their uncer­
tainty estimates are frequently miscalibrated. There is an
open need for methods that can provide reliable uncertainty
quantification for hybrid systems combining learned and
model-based components, and for tools that allow practitio­
ners to diagnose, verify, and explain localization behaviour
to both engineers and non-expert stakeholders.

**Ethical, privacy, and societal considerations.** As
robots increasingly operate in homes, workplaces, and
public spaces, localization and mapping acquire explicit
ethical and legal dimensions. High-resolution geometric
and semantic maps can inadvertently capture and store

of detailed spatial data. Techniques such as on-device pro­

regions, and privacy-preserving map sharing are begin­
ning to appear, but remain far from standard practice. Close
collaboration with legal and ethics communities will be
required to translate emerging regulatory frameworks into
concrete design guidelines and technical mechanisms for
privacy-aware localization.

Persistent localization and mapping in domestic, clinical,
and workplace environments thus raise privacy concerns
that go beyond classical safety and reliability. Survey-based
studies on social robots show that users’ willingness to adopt
such systems depends strongly on how invasively the plat­
form collects and processes personal data: privacy-friendly
configurations lead to significantly higher use intentions
than privacy-invasive ones (Lutz & Tamò-Larrieux 2021).
Concrete incidents underline these concerns. For example,
a widely discussed case involving development versions of
a consumer robot vacuum demonstrated how images cap­
tured for mapping and obstacle avoidance, including highly
intimate scenes, entered cloud-based annotation workflows
and ultimately circulated on social media platforms (Guo
2022). Such episodes illustrate that even ostensibly benign
localization and mapping functions can expose sensitive
information about a person’s home layout, possessions, and
everyday activities.

These risks have motivated a growing line of work on pri­
vacy-preserving localization and SLAM. For camera-based
systems, several approaches aim to retain geometric utility
while obfuscating appearance. Line-cloud map representa­

reduce the amount of recoverable scene detail while still

**5.3 Future outlook and vision**


Looking ahead, robot localization is likely to evolve from a
predominantly geometric estimation problem into a broader
capability that combines geometry, semantics, and highlevel reasoning. The taxonomies in Sects. 2–4 suggest that
future systems will increasingly rely on multi-layer maps,
hybrid estimators that blend learning with probabilistic
inference, and sensor suites that are adapted to specific
operating regimes but able to interoperate through shared
abstractions.


A central step toward this vision is tighter coupling
between localization, mapping, and task-level decisionmaking. Estimators will be expected not only to provide
accurate poses, but also to expose uncertainty, semantic
structure, and failure indicators in a form that planners and



supervisory modules can use. Advances in large language
models (LLMs) and vision-language models (VLMs) pro­
vide additional tools for connecting human instructions,
textual knowledge, and spatial representations, but their
integration must be guided by careful consideration of reli­
ability, safety, and privacy.

Ultimately, progress in robot localization will be mea­
sured not only by reductions in average pose error on
benchmark datasets, but by the ability of systems to oper­
ate robustly, transparently, and responsibly in the diverse
environments where robots are deployed. The research
directions outlined above aim to move the field toward such
systems, in which localization is a reliable, interpretable
component of autonomous behaviour rather than a brittle,
opaque subsystem.
